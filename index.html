DPU on a Supplier Quote: Reading the Unloading Clause Before You Compare It With DDP

A supplier quote that ends in "DPU" looks finished. It names a delivery place, it often names a price that reaches your country, and it can sit in a spreadsheet next to a DDP quote as if the two were the same kind of number. They are not. DPU and DDP both deliver to a named place, but the line between what the seller covers and what the buyer still owes falls in a different spot. This article is about reading that line before you compare prices, with a German delivery as the running example.

Where DPU and DDP actually differ

JTLGO's summary of the two terms is short. DPU focuses on delivery and unloading at a named place. DDP generally includes duty-paid delivery, with import clearance, duties and taxes handled by the seller or logistics provider. In practice, DPU usually places import clearance and duty responsibility on the buyer unless something else is arranged.

That single difference changes what the headline price includes. A DPU price may cover transport to the named place and unloading there, while the import clearance, the duty and the tax remain with you. A DDP price is meant to carry those import costs inside the quote. If you put the two side by side without adjusting for that, the DPU line can look cheaper for the simple reason that part of the landed cost is missing from it.

The most common mistake JTLGO flags is exactly this: assuming DPU includes duties and taxes like DDP. The second is close behind it: accepting a supplier's Incoterm without checking who handles delivery and unloading at the destination. Both mistakes come from reading the three letters instead of the scope behind them. JTLGO's guide to how DPU's unloading-only scope differs from duty-paid delivery sets out the questions in the same order used below.

The four questions to write next to every quote

Before comparing any two China quotes, whether they use DPU, DAP, DDP, CIF, FOB or EXW, write down who handles each of these:

  1. Export from China.
  2. Import clearance at destination.
  3. Duties and taxes.
  4. Unloading and final delivery.

Then add one more check: whether the destination address can actually receive freight under the selected term. A named place that cannot take a delivery, or has no way to unload it, turns a tidy Incoterm into an exception at arrival.

JTLGO also warns against comparing DDP and DPU quotes as if they include the same cost scope, and against hidden costs: a quote can leave customs, duty, tax or final-mile charges outside the headline price. The test is simple. For each of the four lines above, the quote should either include it or say plainly that it does not.

The named place and the delivery responsibility should be written clearly before shipping. "DPU Hamburg" and "DPU, unloaded at consignee warehouse, postcode stated" are different instructions, and only the second can be checked.

What to send when you ask for a route review

A route review is useful only if it receives the same information the supplier used to write the quote. JTLGO asks for:

  • The supplier quote and its Incoterm, the named place, the pickup city, the cargo ready date and the product type.
  • Invoice value, HS code if you know it, carton count, weight, dimensions and packing status.
  • Destination country, delivery address type, unloading constraints and whether you already have a customs broker.
  • Whether duties, taxes, final-mile delivery and unloading must be included in the quote.

The last point is the one buyers skip. If you want a duty-paid comparison, say so; otherwise the review can only tell you what the DPU quote leaves out, not what a DDP version would cost.

A German delivery as the worked example

Germany is a useful case because the import side has several moving parts. JTLGO's Germany page says final cost depends on carton data, cargo type, invoice value, duty and VAT scope, destination postcode and final-mile delivery limits, and that the route has to be screened before export.

Suppose a supplier offers a DPU price to a named place in Germany, and you want to know whether a duty-paid route makes more sense. Under DPU as JTLGO describes it, import clearance and duty would usually fall to you unless arranged otherwise. To compare like with like, a Germany DDP review needs the German postcode, the address type, the delivery deadline, and whether the consignee is a business, a warehouse or a residence. It also needs the supplier city, cargo ready date, carton count, gross weight, packed dimensions, product description and invoice value.

The review may conclude that Germany DDP is not the right answer. JTLGO checks whether DDP is suitable, or whether DAP, air, sea, express or formal customs handling is safer. Air DDP can fit urgent cartons when chargeable weight, invoice value and product type are acceptable; sea DDP can fit heavier or bulkier cargo when a slower route is acceptable. Electronics, batteries, branded goods and other regulated categories should be reviewed before payment. You can start from the China-to-Germany duty-paid route review with the details listed above.

One boundary holds whichever term wins. DDP does not remove product compliance, customs, duty or VAT, declaration or restricted-goods requirements. JTLGO lists treating DDP as a way to bypass accurate declaration as a risk, not a feature. Using DDP for restricted goods without importer, customs or route review is also on its list of mistakes.

What a clean comparison looks like

A useful comparison ends with two rows that describe the same delivery. Each row names the term, the named place, who handles export, import clearance, duties and taxes, unloading and final delivery, and which of those costs are inside the price. If a DPU row still leaves clearance and duty with you, add your estimate or broker cost to it before comparing totals, or ask for a DDP version of the same scope.
